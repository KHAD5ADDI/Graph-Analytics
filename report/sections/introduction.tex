Counting how often an ingredient appears tells us how common it is in a
collection of recipes. It does not tell us which ingredients occur
together. A graph of co-occurrence represents these combinations as
relations. We can then study groups of ingredients and the ingredients
that lie on many shortest paths. At the country level, shared ingredients
also let us compare dish profiles with a common measure.

Our central question is: \emph{What communities and connecting nodes emerge from ingredient-based graphs of sampled recipes and countries?}
We address it in two parts. For ingredients, we ask whether structural
rankings differ from frequency rankings and how much the answer depends
on the weighting used for shortest paths. We call ingredients with high
betweenness brokers. For countries, we ask how communities relate,
descriptively, to United Nations (UN) regions~\cite{unregions}.

The first graph uses recipes from CulinaryDB~\cite{culinarydb}. We start
with a bipartite graph linking recipes to their ingredients and project it
onto ingredients, following a method taught in the course~\cite{quercini2026}.
We then filter the pairs and replace shared-recipe counts with pointwise
mutual information (PMI) weights~\cite{church1990}. Ingredients are nodes,
and retained co-occurrences are edges. PMI measures association relative
to independent occurrence. The second graph uses the national-dishes
dataset~\cite{wunderlich2022data}. Each node is a country. We combine the
ingredients of all recorded dishes for each country into one set and
weight edges by Jaccard similarity. This compares the ingredients shared
by two countries with the ingredients in their union.

Caprioli et al.~\cite{caprioli2025} study networks of ingredient combinations
on CulinaryDB data as culinary fingerprints of cuisines. Our project uses
single ingredients as nodes with PMI weights. We focus on brokers through
betweenness and add a country graph whose communities we compare with UN
regions. We did not use their code.

We follow the course methods~\cite{quercini2026} with
NetworkX~\cite{hagberg2008}. We report the required graph statistics:
node and edge counts, density, connected components, diameter, radius
and average clustering. We study degree, strength and betweenness, and
find communities with Louvain~\cite{blondel2008}, assessed with modularity.
We also find articulation points and bridges. We compare centrality
with recipe frequency using Spearman correlation, compare hop and
$1/\mathrm{PMI}$ distances for betweenness, and vary the graph thresholds.
Two elements go beyond the course: the PMI weights, and a shuffle test of
agreement between country communities and UN regions.

Section~2 presents the data and the two graphs, Section~3 the methods, Section~4 the results, and Section~5 our conclusions.
