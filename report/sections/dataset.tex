Our data are lists of recipes and dishes, not graphs. We turn them into two
graphs that we build and analyse separately. In both, edges are undirected,
because the relation is symmetric: if ingredient $i$ occurs with ingredient
$j$, then $j$ occurs with $i$, and the Jaccard similarity of two ingredient
sets does not depend on their order. Both graphs are simple: there are no
loops and at most one edge between two nodes, so a pair that occurs in many
recipes becomes one weighted edge.

In the \emph{ingredient graph}, a node is an ingredient, an edge joins two
ingredients that occur together in enough recipes, and its weight is the
pointwise mutual information (PMI) of the pair. In the \emph{country graph},
a node is a country, an edge joins two countries whose dishes share enough
ingredients, and its weight is the Jaccard similarity of their ingredient
sets. Table~\ref{tab:stats} gives the required statistics of both graphs. The
subsections below present the sources and the construction of each graph.
